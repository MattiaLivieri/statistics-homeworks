Convergence and Bayesian Inference

Modes of convergence, limit theorems, conditional probability and Bayes’ theorem.

1. Modes of convergence in probability theory

Let Yn be a sequence of real random variables and Y a proposed limit. Convergence can refer to individual realizations, the size of random errors, or the distributions of the variables. The first three modes below compare variables on the same probability space.

ModeMathematical definitionMeaning and role
In probability limn→∞ P(|Yn − Y| > ε) = 0 for every ε > 0. The probability of an error exceeding any fixed tolerance tends to zero. This is the conclusion of the weak LLN and the usual meaning of consistency.
Almost sure (a.s.) P(limn→∞ Yn = Y) = 1. For almost every outcome, the sequence converges along that outcome's entire trajectory. This is the strong LLN.
Mean-square (L²) limn→∞ E[|Yn − Y|²] = 0, for square-integrable variables. The expected squared error vanishes. It is useful for evaluating estimators through mean squared error.
In distribution limn→∞ FYₙ(t) = FY(t) at every continuity point of the limiting cumulative distribution function (CDF). The probability laws converge; the variables need not share a probability space. The CLT gives this kind of limit.

Relationships between the modes

Almost sure ⇒ In probability ⇒ In distribution
Mean-square (L²) ⇒ In probability ⇒ In distribution

Almost sure convergence controls the eventual behavior of almost every trajectory. Mean-square convergence implies convergence in probability by the inequality P(|Yn−Y| > ε) ≤ E[|Yn−Y|²]/ε². The reverse implications generally fail, except that convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability to that constant. Almost sure convergence and L² convergence do not imply each other in general.

  • L² without a.s. convergence: take independent Bernoulli variables Yn with P(Yn = 1) = 1/n. Their squared error relative to zero has expectation 1/n → 0. However, since the sum of these probabilities diverges, the second Borel–Cantelli lemma gives infinitely many ones almost surely. They do not converge almost surely to zero.
  • A.s. without L² convergence: let U be uniform on (0, 1), and Yn = n·1{U≤1/n²}, where 1 denotes an indicator. For almost every U, the variable is eventually zero, but E[Yn²] = 1 for all n.
  • Distribution without probability convergence: if Y is equally likely to be −1 or 1 and Yn = −Y, their distributions are identical. Nevertheless, |Yn−Y| = 2, so there is no convergence in probability to Y.

Why √n appears: additivity of variance and independence

Let X1, …, Xn be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations with mean μ and variance σ², where 0 < σ² < ∞. Define their sum Sn and sample mean X̄n:

Sn = ∑i=1n Xi,   X̄n = Sn/n.

For any finite-variance observations,

Var(Sn) = ∑i=1n Var(Xi) + 2∑i<j Cov(Xi, Xj).

Independence makes the covariance terms zero. Pairwise uncorrelatedness is sufficient for this variance calculation. With common variance σ², the sum has variance nσ² and standard deviation σ√n. The mean has variance σ²/n and standard deviation σ/√n.

Centering removes the expected growth of the sum, while dividing by √n compensates for the growth of its standard deviation. Dependence can change this scale: if every Xi equals the same random variable W, then Var(Sn) = n²Var(W). Averaging those identical observations does not reduce uncertainty. Zero covariance alone therefore explains the variance formula, but does not establish the usual independent-sample CLT.

Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

For i.i.d. observations with E[|X1|] < ∞, the weak law states that X̄n converges in probability to μ; the strong law states that X̄n converges almost surely to μ.

When the variance is also finite, the sample mean is unbiased, and

E[(X̄n − μ)²] = Var(X̄n) = σ²/n → 0.
P(|X̄n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) → 0.

The first expression proves convergence in L². The second, obtained by Chebyshev’s inequality, proves the finite-variance weak LLN. The i.i.d. weak and strong laws also hold with a finite first absolute moment even when the variance is infinite; the L² argument requires a finite second moment.

Central Limit Theorem (CLT) and statistical inference

For i.i.d. observations with finite, positive variance, the classical CLT states

Zn = (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) = √n(X̄n − μ)/σ.
Zn → Z in distribution, where Z ∼ N(0, 1).

The LLN gives consistency of the sample mean, whereas the CLT describes the limiting distribution of its rescaled fluctuations. The CLT concerns probability laws and by itself does not imply that individual standardized trajectories converge.

In statistical inference, the standard error of the sample mean is σ/√n. For example, if σ = 10, increasing n from 25 to 100 reduces the standard error from 2 to 1. For large independent samples, an approximate 95% confidence interval for μ is

x̄ ± 1.96 s/√n,

where s is the sample standard deviation. Under the stated assumptions, the CLT and consistency of s justify an approximate 95% repeated-sampling coverage. Thus √n determines the rate at which uncertainty decreases and explains why halving the standard error generally requires four times as many independent observations.

2. Conditional probability, Bayes’ theorem and statistical inference

Conditional probability and Bayes’ theorem

Conditional probability evaluates an event A when the information B is known. If P(B) > 0,

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B).

For independent events, conditioning on B leaves P(A) unchanged. In statistics, conditional probabilities describe how observations are distributed for a given hypothesis or model parameter. They can also be estimated from observed frequencies: the empirical conditional probability is the number of observations satisfying both A and B divided by the number satisfying B, provided that this count is positive.

Let H denote a hypothesis and D the observed data. Expressing P(H ∩ D) in both possible ways gives Bayes’ theorem:

P(H | D) = P(D | H) P(H)/P(D),   P(D) > 0.
ComponentMeaning
Prior: P(H)Information about the hypothesis before using the new data.
Likelihood: P(D | H)How compatible the observed data are with each candidate hypothesis.
Posterior: P(H | D)The updated probability of the hypothesis after incorporating the data.

The denominator P(D), called the evidence, normalizes the updated probabilities. For mutually exclusive, exhaustive hypotheses Hj, it is

P(D) = ∑j P(D | Hj) P(Hj).

For a continuous parameter θ with prior density π(θ) and likelihood L(θ; d) = f(d | θ), the corresponding update, provided the denominator is positive and finite, is

π(θ | d) = L(θ; d) π(θ) / ∫ L(t; d) π(t) dt.
Posterior ∝ Likelihood × Prior.

The likelihood holds the observed data fixed and varies the parameter. It is not generally a normalized probability distribution over that parameter. Bayesian inference uses the posterior for estimating unknown quantities, expressing uncertainty and predicting new observations. A posterior can become the prior for a later update with new data: when datasets are conditionally independent given θ, their likelihoods multiply.

Example 1 — Updating the probability of a defective component

Consider a hypothetical quality-control system. Let H mean that a component is defective and D mean that an inspection flags it. Suppose past information gives a defect probability of 1%. A defective item is flagged with probability 0.95, and a non-defective item with probability 0.05.

P(D) = 0.95 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.059.
P(H | D) = (0.95 × 0.01)/0.059 = 0.1610 ≈ 16.10%.

For 10,000 hypothetical components, the expected counts are 95 flagged defective items and 495 flagged non-defective items. Based on these counts, 95 out of 590 alerts correspond to defects. The posterior is very different from the inspection's 95% detection rate because the prior probability of a defect is low.

The same positive result means something different in a population with a different base rate. A test with fewer false positives provides stronger evidence. This updating is useful for deciding which components deserve further inspection, but its conclusions depend on reliable estimates of both defect prevalence and inspection performance.

Example 2 — Estimating an unknown coin bias

Let θ be the unknown probability of heads. Conditional on θ, suppose the tosses are independent Bernoulli trials. Observing k heads in n tosses gives the binomial likelihood

L(θ; k) = C(n,k) θk(1−θ)n−k,   0 ≤ θ ≤ 1,

where C(n,k) is the binomial coefficient. Use a Beta(a, b) prior, with positive parameters a and b and density proportional to θa−1(1−θ)b−1. Multiplying it by the likelihood adds the observed head and tail counts to its two parameters:

Prior: θ ∼ Beta(a, b).
Posterior: θ | k ∼ Beta(a+k, b+n−k).
Posterior mean = (a+k)/(a+b+n).

Compare a uniform prior, Beta(1,1), with Beta(10,10), which places more probability near a fair coin. Both priors have mean 0.5. The table gives the posterior distribution and its mean under two sample sizes with an observed head proportion of 0.8.

With ten tosses, prior concentration has a substantial effect. For the same success proportion and 100 tosses, the results are closer to 0.8 and to each other. Algebraically, the posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean a/(a+b) and the observed proportion k/n, with weights proportional to a+b and n. With fixed positive prior parameters and an increasingly large representative sample, the data receive more weight.

The posterior mean provides a point estimate of θ and, under the same model, the predictive probability of heads on the next toss. This illustrates how Bayes’ theorem turns a distribution over an unknown parameter into an updated estimate and prediction.

Advantages and limitations

Bayesian inference allows existing knowledge to be combined with new observations, provides probability distributions over unknown parameters, and supports sequential learning, credible intervals and prediction. Informative priors can stabilize estimates when data are scarce.

However, conclusions may be sensitive to the selected prior, especially for small samples. An unrealistic prior or a misspecified likelihood can produce misleading posteriors. A discrete hypothesis assigned zero prior probability cannot gain posterior probability through ordinary updating, and normalization may be computationally difficult for complex models. Comparing reasonable priors and checking the likelihood assumptions are therefore important when interpreting results.

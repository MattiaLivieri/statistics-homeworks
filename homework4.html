Welford’s Recurrences and Sampling Variability

One-pass statistics and an experiment with penetration scores from 0 to 10.

1. Welford’s recurrence formulas

After observing x1, …, xn, let Mn be their mean and SSn their sum of squared deviations from that mean:

Mn = (1/n) ∑i=1n xi
SSn = ∑i=1n (xi − Mn)2

From the mean update to the sum of squares

For n ≥ 2, separate the new observation from the first n − 1. With δ = xn − Mn−1, Knuth’s mean update is

Mn = [(n − 1)Mn−1 + xn]/n = Mn−1 + δ/n.

Put d = Mn − Mn−1 = δ/n. Recenter each of the old deviations at Mn by subtracting d. Expanding the square gives

SSn = ∑i=1n−1 [(xi − Mn−1) − d]2 + (xn − Mn)2
= SSn−1 + (n − 1)d2 + (xn − Mn)2.

The cross term vanishes because ∑i=1n−1 (xi − Mn−1) = 0. Now xn − Mn = δ(n − 1)/n. Substituting into the last two terms yields δ2(n − 1)/n, so the update is

SSn = SSn−1 + (xn − Mn−1)(xn − Mn).

Variance recurrences

Since the sample variance is sn2 = SSn/(n − 1), substitute SSn−1 = (n − 2)sn−12 and divide by n − 1. For the empirical variance vn = SSn/n, substitute SSn−1 = (n − 1)vn−1 and divide by n instead.

sn2 = [(n − 2)/(n − 1)]sn−12 + δ2/n,   n ≥ 3.
vn = [(n − 1)/n]vn−1 + [(n − 1)/n2]δ2,   n ≥ 2.

At the first observation, M1 = x1 and SS1 = v1 = 0. The sample variance first exists at n = 2, where s22 = (x2 − x1)2/2.

JavaScript implementation

function updateWelford(s, x) {
  const delta = x - s.mean;
  s.mean += delta / (++s.n);
  s.ss += delta * (x - s.mean);
}

Initialize the state with { n: 0, mean: 0, ss: 0 }. The three statements compute the old deviation, update the count and mean, then update SS using the new mean. Read s.ss / (s.n - 1) when s.n >= 2. Each update uses constant memory and requires one pass over the data. For the scores 2, 5, 8, the final mean is 5, SS is 18, and the sample variance is 9.

2. Sampling mean and variance

Score model and experiment

Each hacker attempts n systems. The score for one attempt, X, is uniform on the eleven integers 0, 1, …, 10: P(X = j) = 1/11 for every j in that range. Attempts are modeled as independent with this same distribution, both within and across hackers.

μ = (1/11) ∑j=010 j = 5.
σ2 = (1/11) ∑j=010 (j − 5)2 = 10.

For hacker h, the program feeds one score at a time into Welford’s update. It records the resulting mean Mh,n and sample variance sh,n2 at n = 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500 and 1,000. The smaller samples are prefixes of the same trajectory. At each size it averages the estimates over the m hackers:

AM(n) = (1/m) ∑h=1m Mh,n,   AV(n) = (1/m) ∑h=1m sh,n2.

To measure how much the individual estimates differ, it also computes their standard deviations across hackers, using denominator m − 1. The default run uses 5,000 hackers and a fixed seed so the experiment can be reproduced.

What theory predicts

The sample mean has E[Mn] = μ and Var(Mn) = σ2/n. To see why the sample variance also has the correct expected value, write

SSn = ∑i=1n (Xi − μ)2 − n(Mn − μ)2,
E[SSn] = nσ2 − n(σ2/n) = (n − 1)σ2.

Thus E[sn2] = σ2 when we divide by n − 1. Both across-hacker averages are therefore centered on their respective targets, for every tested n.

The fourth central moment of the scores is μ4 = (1/11)∑j=010(j − 5)4 = 178. The sampling variance of sn2 gives a second measure of estimator precision:

Var(Mn) = 10/n.
Var(sn2) = [μ4 − ((n − 3)/(n − 1))σ4]/n = [78 + 200/(n − 1)]/n.

The theoretical standard deviations in the table are the square roots of these two quantities. The first describes the spread of the sample means; the second describes the spread of the sample variance estimates.

Conclusions and relevance to inference

In the displayed run, the average sample mean is 4.9960 at n = 5 and 4.9997 at n = 1,000 (target: 5). The corresponding average sample variances are 10.0150 and 9.9975 (target: 10). Across the same sizes, the standard deviation of the sample means changes from 1.4298 to 0.1010, and that of the sample variances changes from 5.0513 to 0.2749.

Increasing n makes the individual estimates more precise. Their sampling variances are of order 1/n, so their standard deviations are of order 1/√n. The population variance stays at 10 while the estimates of it become less dispersed. Unbiasedness and vanishing sampling variance together give consistency. Individual estimates and finite simulation averages can still fluctuate between adjacent sample sizes.

In real data, μ and σ2 are usually unknown. Unbiasedness tells us that repeated samples target the right values; sampling variability tells us how precise one estimate is. Those two properties help choose a sample size and quantify uncertainty, for example with standard errors. Increasing m makes the simulation averages more stable, since averaging independent estimates divides their variance by m. It does not make one hacker’s estimate more precise at fixed n.
